\begin{abstract}
Let $h(N)$ be the maximal size of a Sidon set in $\{1,\dots,N\}$. Erd\H{o}s asked whether $h(N)=N^{1/2}+O(1)$ (Guy C9); the weaker
$h(N)=N^{1/2}+O_\varepsilon(N^\varepsilon)$ is Erd\H{o}s problem \#30. Both questions remain open, and we do not settle them. We study dilates of Singer perfect difference sets $S$
($|S|=q+1$, modulus $m=q^2+q+1$) and the number $Z$ of points of a random dilate in a window of length $Cq$.
\emph{Proved:} (i) if the factorial moments of $Z$ converge to the Poisson ones for every fixed order and every $C$, then $h(N)-N^{1/2}$ is unbounded
(Proposition~\ref{prop:red}; experts regard this reduction as folklore, and we give a written proof); (ii) for a ratio $\rho$, the number $N(\rho)$ of triples of $S$ with $c-a=\rho(b-a)$
is the number of $\mathbb F_q$-points of a twisted form of a curve that does not depend on $q$, so $N(\rho)/E-1=O_\rho(q^{-1/2})$ for 17 ratio orbits certified by exact computation (Theorem~\ref{thm:curve});
(iii) for prime $m$, $\sum_\rho N(\rho)^2=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}(|S\cap(\lambda S+\mu)|)_3$; (iv) $3k\mid N(\rho)$ for $q=p^k$, and a resonance theorem for cube $q=r^3$: $N(\rho)=r(q+1-e)$,
$e\in\{0,1\}$, for the six ratios of the $S_3$-orbit of $r+r^{-1}$ ($m$ prime); (v) for prime $q\ge5$ and every unit $\lambda$, the per-dilation third moment
$\sum_\mu(|S\cap(\lambda S+\mu)|)_3$ equals $P(\lambda)-(q-2)^2$, where $P(\lambda)$ counts solutions of $c\,y^\lambda+d(1-y)^\lambda=1$ ($y\in\mathbb F_{q^3}$, $c,d\in\mathbb F_q^\times$);
it is $0$ at $\lambda=2,-1$ (the classical conic fact), $(q-2)(q+1)$ at $\lambda=3$ ($q\equiv2\pmod3$), and $(q-2)(q+1)+b_q$ at $\lambda=-2$, where $b_q$ is the $q$-th
coefficient of the CM newform $\eta(2\tau)^3\eta(6\tau)^3$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}; one step uses an evaluation of a finite-field hypergeometric value due to Ono, in the form of Barman and Kalita).
(vi) For every prime power $q=p^n$ with $p\ge7$, $\sum_\mu(|S\cap(4S+\mu)|)_3=(q-2)(q+1)+b_q$, where $b_q=\alpha^n+\beta^n$ and $\alpha,\beta$ are the Frobenius eigenvalues at $p$ of the Galois representation of the same CM form (for $n>1$ this is not the coefficient $a_q$); so $P(4)=P(-2)$ for primes $q\ge7$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:four}; the proof is computer-assisted, uses a singular K3 surface and Livn\'e's modularity criterion, and was reviewed by AI instances only).
\emph{Observed} (registrations are self-attested, see Section~\ref{sec:lim}): to first order the $k$-th factorial moment of $Z$ falls short of the random value by
$\binom k3P_3+\binom k4P_4$, where $P_3,P_4$ are the window probabilities of 3-term progressions and parallelograms ($k=3,\dots,6$ at $C=2$ on 129 primes; $k=7$ at $C=2$
and $k=3,\dots,8$ at $C=4$ on 64 of them); and $\sum_\rho(N(\rho)-E)^2$ is close to the order of the multiplier group times $\sum_\rho E$ for prime $q$ and non-cube prime powers,
while most of the excess at cube $q$ is explained by (iv).
\emph{What remains:} a negative answer would follow from Poisson convergence of all fixed-order factorial moments. For order $3$ this reduces to a variance bound, which by (iii) and (v)
is a statement about the average of $P(\lambda)$ over all $\lambda$; for order $\ge4$ we have no such reduction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background}
A set $A\subset\mathbb Z$ is Sidon if all sums $a+b$ ($a\le b$) are distinct. Erd\H{o}s and Tur\'an \cite{ET} showed
$h(N)\le N^{1/2}+O(N^{1/4})$; Singer's construction \cite{Si} with the Baker--Harman--Pintz prime-gap theorem \cite{BHP} gives
$h(N)\ge N^{1/2}-O(N^{0.2625})$; the constant in the $N^{1/4}$ term has since been improved several times (Balogh--F\"uredi--Roy
\cite{BFR} and later work listed at \cite{EP}). Erd\H{o}s offered USD~500 for deciding whether
$h(N)=N^{1/2}+O(1)$ \cite{Guy,MO}. The site \cite{EP} lists the weaker question $h(N)=N^{1/2}+O_\varepsilon(N^\varepsilon)$ as
problem \#30 (USD~1000), with the $O(1)$ form as a remark; the evidence below concerns the $O(1)$ question only.
A Sidon set $A$ modulo $m$ with $|A|=k$, cut at its largest cyclic gap $G$, gives a Sidon set of integers in an interval of length
$m-G$; for Bose sets \cite{Bo} ($m=q^2-1$, $k=q$) this gives $k-\sqrt{N}\approx G/(2q)$. Martin and O'Bryant \cite{MO} note that
the question would be answered (excess unbounded) if Bose sets had gaps that are not $O(q)$. Dilating $A$ by a unit $t$ preserves
the Sidon property and moves the gaps; the use of dilates was suggested in B.~Green's comment on \cite{Go} (see also \cite{Gr}; earlier computer searches of dilates and cuts: \cite{Z,RD}), and Shakan \cite{Sh}
proved that every $A\subset\mathbb F_p$ with $|A|>1$ has a dilate with a gap of at least $2p/|A|-2$.
Throughout, ``registered'' and ``blind'' mean that a plan file and its SHA-256 hash were recorded locally before the computation; these records are self-attested (there is no third-party timestamp), and later amendments to a plan are listed in the release.

\section{Dilate gaps of Bose sets}
For every prime $q$ in the ranges below we built $\mathrm{Bose}(q)\subset\mathbb Z/(q^2-1)$, checked the Sidon property, and computed
$G^*(q)=\max_t G(tA)$ over one multiplier per class $\{\pm t,\pm tq\}$. Positive control: $q=829$ gives excess $10.2797$, matching
Zhang's bound $K(p_{145})>10.279$ ($p_{145}=829$) \cite{Z}.
\begin{itemize}
\item $101\le q\le2003$ (279 primes): a $\log q$ law fitted on $q\le1000$, $g^*\approx-6.92+3.63\ln q$, gives held-out ($q>1000$)
RMSE $1.22$, against $4.25$ for a constant fitted on the same data. (Saturating bounded models were not tested.)
\item Blind test (law registered before computing): 48 primes $2011\le q\le5881$ (every 10th prime in $(2003,6000]$): RMSE $1.52$
(log) against $7.34$ (constant), mean signed error $-0.57$. The largest $q$ ($5881$) gives a Sidon set of size $5881$ in $[1,34{,}453{,}078]$ with excess $11.33$; the largest excess in
this set is $13.18$ ($q=5039$).
\item Null model: random $q$-subsets of $\mathbb Z/(q^2-1)$ show the same growth (Bose slightly lower at 15 of 20 primes), so the
$\log q$ growth is random-set behaviour; counts of 3-term relations $e_1L_1+e_2L_2+e_3L_3\equiv0$ among the discrete logarithms are at
the random level (mean per-$q$ ratio $0.983$ over 11 primes $101\le q\le383$; percentile bootstrap over the primes, 95\% interval
$[0.96,1.00]$).
\end{itemize}

\section{The moment route for Singer sets}
Let $S$ be a Singer set \cite{Si} ($|S|=n=q+1$, $m=q^2+q+1$), $\ell=Cq$, and $Z_{t,s}=\#\{a\in S:(ta+s)\bmod m\in[0,\ell)\}$ for
units $t$ and shifts $s$. Let $F_k$ be the average of the falling factorial $(Z)_k$ and $H_k=(n)_k(\ell)_k/(m)_k$ its value for a
uniformly random $n$-subset; $R_k=F_k/H_k-1$.
\begin{proposition}
For every unit $t$, $\operatorname{Var}_s Z_{t,s}=q\ell(m-\ell)/m^2$ and $R_2=0$. If $m$ is prime, averaging over all units,
\[ R_3=\sum_{\rho\ne0,1}\Bigl(\frac{N(\rho)}{E}-1\Bigr)\frac{A(\rho)}{\sum_\sigma A(\sigma)},\qquad
N(\rho)=\#\{(a,b,c)\in S^3\ \text{distinct}: c-a\equiv\rho(b-a)\},\ E=\frac{(n)_3}{m-2},\]
where $A(\rho)$ is the average over $u\in(\mathbb Z/m)^\times$ of the number of windows $[s,s+\ell)$ containing $\{0,u,u\rho\}$, and
$\sum_\sigma A(\sigma)=\ell(\ell-1)(\ell-2)/(m-1)$.
\end{proposition}
(The first part holds because every nonzero residue is exactly one difference of $tS$ and $(n)_2=m-1$; for the second, $m$ prime
lets every triple be written as $(a,b,c)$ with $c-a=\rho(b-a)$, and averaging over all units $u$ sends it to every pair $(u,u\rho)$.) Since a perfect difference set
has $N(-1)=N(2)=N(1/2)=0$ and $A(\rho)\approx\ell^2/(2m)$ for these $\rho$, the three forbidden ratios contribute
$-3/(2Cq)(1+O(1/q))$ (within $0.2\%$ for $q\ge293$, $1.2\%$ at $q=41$).
\begin{itemize}
\item $R_k\sim c_k(C)/q$ (moments estimated from 4000 sampled multipliers): registered blind test for $k=4$ on 11 new primes
$409\le q\le601$ ($C=2$: RMSE $0.0028$ for $c/q$ against $0.0238$ for a constant; $C=4$: $0.0013$ against $0.0112$); the same is seen
descriptively for $k=3,5,6$, with $|c_k|$ growing quickly in $k$.
\item Exact over all units: registered tests on 18 primes $293\le q\le1373$ ($m$ prime) and, from a sealed data set opened under a
frozen plan, 17 primes $1559\le q\le2969$: the mean of $C\,qR_3$ is between $-1.45$ and $-1.41$, resp.\ between $-1.47$ and $-1.46$,
inside the registered bands around $-3/2$ for all $C\in\{2,3,4,5,8\}$. The first test could not separate $-3/2$ from an earlier
empirical value $-1.17$; the second band ($[-1.59,-1.31]$) excludes it.
\item The RMS deviation $D_{\rm mid}$ of $N(\rho)/E$ over ratios of height $3$--$6$: the registered decay test on $293\le q\le1373$
alone \emph{failed} (slope $-0.13\pm0.17$, outside the band $[-0.65,-0.35]$; few independent values per $q$). A second test, frozen
before opening the sealed data but pooling them with the already seen first set, gives slope $-0.504\pm0.098$ over $293\le q\le2969$
(the sealed set alone: $-1.28\pm0.39$, i.e.\ noisy); $\sqrt q\,D_{\rm mid}$ lies between $0.49$ and $1.84$. Over all ratios the exponent is $-0.50$ in both
sets, with $\sqrt q\,D_{\rm all}\approx1.73$ for Singer sets against about $1.0$ for random sets, consistent with counting noise
inflated by the size-3 orbits of the multiplier $q$. These data are consistent with, but do not establish, a $q^{-1/2}$ law.
\end{itemize}
Sections~4--8 and~\ref{sec:rem} (new in Version~2; Sections~\ref{sec:orb}--\ref{sec:resblind} are new in Version~3) turn the heuristic of Version~1 into a theorem for each fixed ratio, give exact identities,
explain the constants $c_k$, and state the reduction precisely.

\section{The reduction}\label{sec:red}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:red}
Fix $C>0$ and let $\ell=\lfloor Cq\rfloor$. Suppose that for every fixed $k\ge1$, $R_k=R_k(q,C)\to0$ as $q\to\infty$ through primes.
Then for all large $q$ some dilate of $S$ has an empty arc of length $\ell$, and $\limsup_{N\to\infty}(h(N)-N^{1/2})\ge(C+1)/2$.
In particular, if this holds for every $C$, then $h(N)-N^{1/2}$ is unbounded.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose $(t,s)$ uniformly. Since $Z\le n$, inclusion--exclusion gives $\Pr(Z=0)=\sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^kF_k/k!$, and the Bonferroni
inequalities give $\Pr(Z=0)\ge\sum_{k=0}^{2K+1}(-1)^kF_k/k!$ for every $K$. Since $H_k=(n)_k(\ell)_k/(m)_k\to C^k$, the hypothesis gives
$F_k\to C^k$ for each fixed $k$. Choose $K$ with $\bigl|\sum_{k\le2K+1}(-1)^kC^k/k!-e^{-C}\bigr|<e^{-C}/2$; then $\Pr(Z=0)>0$ for all
large $q$, so some dilate $tS$ misses a window of length $\ell$. Rotating, $tS$ lies in an interval of length $m-\ell$; a Sidon set
modulo $m$ is a Sidon set of integers, so we get $q+1$ elements in $[1,N]$ with $N=m-\ell=q^2+(1-C)q+O(1)$ and
$q+1-N^{1/2}=(C+1)/2+O(1/q)$.
\end{proof}
The cutting step is classical (Section~1). The reduction of unbounded excess to the convergence of fixed-order factorial moments of
window counts is regarded as folklore by experts (private communication); we did not find a written proof and include one here.

\section{Universal curves: a theorem for each fixed ratio}
Write $S=\{i:g^i\in L\}$ with $g$ a generator of $\mathbb F_{q^3}^\times$ and $L$ a 2-dimensional $\mathbb F_q$-subspace. For
$\rho=u/v$ put $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(u-v,v,-u)$, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0$, and let
\[C_{u,v}:\qquad s^\alpha r^\beta t^\gamma=1,\qquad (s-1)^\alpha(r-1)^\beta(t-1)^\gamma=1 .\]
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:curve}
(a) For $q$ prime, $N(\rho)$ equals the number of $\mathbb F_q$-points of a twisted form of $C_{u,v}$ off its diagonal $s=r=t$, up to
$O_\rho(1)$; the twist is Frobenius composed with the order-3 map $s\mapsto1-1/s$.
(b) For $\rho\in\{-1,2,1/2\}$, $C_{u,v}$ is the diagonal, so $N(\rho)=0$.
(c) If $C_{u,v}$ minus the diagonal is absolutely irreducible over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$, then $N(\rho)/E-1=O_\rho(q^{-1/2})$ for all
primes $q>q_0(\rho)$.
(d) The hypothesis of (c) holds for every ratio of height $\le4$ other than $-1,2,1/2$ (five orbits) and for the orbits (under $\rho\mapsto1-\rho,1/\rho$) of
$-5,-5/2,-5/4,-6,-6/5,-7,-7/2,-7/6,-8,-8/3,-8/5,-8/7$: 17 orbits in all. With (b), 18 of the 22 orbits of height $\le8$ are settled.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
(a) With $x=g^a$, $y=g^b$, $z=g^c$ the condition is $x^{u-v}z^{v}y^{-u}\in\mathbb F_q^\times$. Parametrise $P(L)\cong\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_q)$
by $x=e+sf$; the three conjugate linear forms $e^{q^i}+sf^{q^i}$ have distinct roots, and a M\"obius change of variable over
$\mathbb F_{q^3}$ sending them to $\infty,0,1$ turns $w\in\mathbb F_q^\times$, i.e.\ $w=w^q=w^{q^2}$, into the two equations of
$C_{u,v}$ (the constants cancel because $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0$); Frobenius becomes the stated twist. (b) For exponents $(-2,1,1)$ the
equations give $r+t=2s$ and $rt=s^2$. (c) Absolute irreducibility over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ persists modulo all large primes; apply the
Weil bound for singular curves \cite{AP} to a $p$-independent projective model; excluded points are $O_\rho(1)$ and $E=q+O(1)$.
(d) Eliminating one variable by a resultant gives a plane curve $G$; we exhibit smooth points of $G$ over number fields whose degrees
have $\gcd1$ (or one quadratic or cubic field over which $G$ stays irreducible). A geometric component through a smooth $K$-point is
defined over a subfield of $K$, so the number of components divides every such degree and equals $1$. Birationality of the projection
is checked by subresultants. Exact arithmetic in SymPy; certificates and code are released. A separate AI review checked (a)--(c)
and the certificate logic, and confirmed (a) by exact counts for $q=5,7,11,13$.
\end{proof}
Numerically, the genus of $C_{u,v}$ appears to grow at least quadratically in the height (for example $(n-3)(n-4)/2$ for exponents $(-n,1,n-1)$, $n\le6$,
by Riemann--Hurwitz with numerical monodromy), so Theorem~\ref{thm:curve} alone does not give $R_3\to0$; see Section~\ref{sec:rem}.
Four further orbits ($-5/3,-7/3,-7/4,-7/5$) exceeded our 10-hour certification budget; none failed.

\section{Exact identities}
Let $I_\lambda(\mu)=|S\cap(\lambda S+\mu)|$ and let $m$ be prime. Two ordered triples of $S$ with the same ratio determine a unique affine map
$x\mapsto\lambda x+\mu$ taking one to the other, hence
\[\sum_{\rho\ne0,1}N(\rho)^2=\sum_{\lambda\in(\mathbb Z/m)^\times}\ \sum_{\mu}\bigl(I_\lambda(\mu)\bigr)_3,\]
which we also checked exactly for $q=5,17,41,59$. For prime $q$ each inner sum $\sum_\mu(I_\lambda(\mu))_3$ is an explicit point count over
$\mathbb F_{q^3}$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:I3} in Section~\ref{sec:gal}). Since every nonzero residue is a unique difference, $\sum_\mu(I_\lambda(\mu))_2=m-1$
for every $\lambda$: second moments are exactly Poisson. For the multipliers $\lambda\in\{1,q,q^2\}$, $\lambda S$ is a translate of
$S$ and one $\mu$ gives $I=n$, contributing exactly $3(n)_3$. Under $x\mapsto x^\lambda$, a dilate by $\lambda$ whose base-$q$ digits
have sum $d$ maps the line $L$ into a curve of degree $d$, so $I_\lambda\le d$ by B\'ezout unless $L$ lies on that curve (this does not happen for $d=2$ and odd $q$); for $d=2$ (for example $\lambda=-1$, a
conic) $(I_\lambda)_3=0$. Numerically, the non-multiplier $\lambda$ with $\sum_\mu(I_\lambda(\mu))_3=0$ are exactly the nine elements
$\{-1,2,1/2\}\cdot\{1,q,q^2\}$ for $q=41,59,167,173,293,383$ (all $\lambda$ computed); the arc and oval facts behind $-1$ and $2$ are classical
\cite{JV,Pi}.

\section{Higher moments: the forbidden-structure law (blind tests)}
A Sidon set contains no 3-term progression and no non-trivial parallelogram $x+y=z+w$. Let $P_3(\ell)$, resp.\ $P_4(\ell)$, be the
probability that an ordered distinct triple of positions in $[0,\ell)$ contains a 3-term progression, resp.\ that an ordered distinct
quadruple is a parallelogram; $P_3\sim3/(2\ell)$ and $P_4\sim2/\ell$. The first-order prediction is
\[R_k\approx-\Bigl[\tbinom k3P_3(\ell)+\tbinom k4P_4(\ell)\Bigr]\sim-\frac{\tfrac32\binom k3+2\binom k4}{Cq}.\]
Two registered blind tests (blind for $k\ge4$ in the first, for all $k$ in the second) used plans frozen with a recorded SHA-256 before computing (self-attested; two amendments made before any computation are listed in the release), 3000 sampled unit classes per $q$, and the
inverse-variance pooled ratio measured/predicted. On 65 primes $607\le q\le1499$ with $C=2$, $k=3,\dots,6$ gave $1.02,1.06,1.06,1.05$
(all 95\% intervals contain $1$). On the other 64 primes of that range, $C=2$ gave $0.97,0.97,0.97,0.97,1.01$ for $k=3,\dots,7$ (the
$k=8$ cell is void because its null control failed), and $C=4$ gave $0.95,0.98,1.00,1.00,1.00,1.01$ for $k=3,\dots,8$. Random sets were
within $3$ standard errors of $0$ in every other cell. So no new forbidden structure appears up to $8$ points, and $c_k\sim k^4$ explains the fast growth
noted in Version~1.

\section{Variance law and prime powers}
\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:var}
$\sum_{\rho}(N(\rho)-E)^2=|G|\,\sum_\rho E\,(1+o(1))$, where $|G|$ is the order of the multiplier group ($|G|=3$ for prime $q$, $|G|=3k$ for
$q=p^k$), the sums run over $\rho$ with $\rho$ and $\rho-1$ units, and $E$ is the mean of $N(\rho)$ over these $\rho$
(equal to $(n)_3/(m-2)$ when $m$ is prime).
\end{conjecture}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:orb} (Section~\ref{sec:orb}), $3k\mid N(\rho)$ for every prime power $q=p^k$. For prime $q$ with $m$ prime, the ratio has mean $2.998$ (s.d.\ $0.008$, range $[2.979,3.019]$) over 37 primes

\begin{proposition}[conditional]
If $\sum_\rho(N(\rho)-E)^2\ll q^3$, then $R_3=O\bigl(\sqrt{\log q/q}\,\bigr)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the Proposition of Section~3, $R_3=\sum_\rho(N/E-1)(w(\rho)-\bar w)$ with $w=A/\sum A$, because $\sum_\rho N(\rho)=(n)_3$ makes the
uniform part vanish. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $|R_3|\le E^{-1}\bigl(\sum_\rho(N-E)^2\bigr)^{1/2}\|w-\bar w\|_2$, and lattice-point counting
for $\{(x,y):y\equiv\rho x\}$ in $(-\ell,\ell)^2$ gives $\|w-\bar w\|_2\ll\sqrt{\log q}/q$.
\end{proof}

\section{Orbits of the multiplier group}\label{sec:orb}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:orb}
Let $q=p^k$ and let $N(\rho)$ count triples $(a,b,c)\in S^3$ with $b-a$ a unit and $c-a=\rho(b-a)$ (for prime $m$ this is the count of Section~3).
Then $3k\mid N(\rho)$ for every $\rho$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the multiplier theorem, some translate of $S$ is fixed by multiplication by $p$ \cite{Ha,MR}. $N$ is translation invariant, so assume $pS=S$.
We have $p^{3k}-1=(q-1)m$. If $p^j\equiv1\pmod m$ with $0<j<3k$, then $m\mid p^d-1$ for $d=\gcd(j,3k)\le 2k$, which is impossible because $0<p^d-1<m$.
So $p$ has order $3k$ modulo $m$. The map $(a,b,c)\mapsto(pa,pb,pc)$ preserves the set of triples. A triple fixed by $p^j$, $0<j<3k$, would give
$(p^j-1)(b-a)=0$ with $b-a$ a unit, which is impossible. So the action is free and every orbit has $3k$ elements.
\end{proof}
This is elementary and probably known. The order statement and the freeness step are machine-checked in Lean~4 with Mathlib
(files \texttt{C9Order.lean}, \texttt{C9Orbit.lean}; standard axioms only). The multiplier theorem itself is not formalised.
So Conjecture~\ref{conj:var} says that $M=N/3k$ is Poisson-like. This holds for non-cube $q$ (Table~\ref{tab:pp}) but fails for cube $q$; the next two sections explain most of this failure.

\section{A resonance theorem for cube $q$}\label{sec:res}
Let $q=r^3$, where $r$ is a prime power. Let $F=\mathbb F_q$ and $K=\mathbb F_{q^3}=\mathbb F_{r^9}$. Write $\tau(u)=u^r$ and $\varphi=\tau^3$ (the $q$-Frobenius).
Let $L=\ker\mathrm{Tr}_{K/F}$, so that $S=\{i: g^i\in L\}$. Since $\tau$ commutes with $\varphi$, we have $\tau(L)=L$. Moreover $\varphi$ is $F$-linear on $L$ with $\varphi^3=1$.
Since $r^9=q^3\equiv1$ and $r^3=q\not\equiv1\pmod m$, $r$ has order $9$ modulo $m$. Put $\rho_1=r+r^{-1}\bmod m$.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:res}
Let $x\in P(L)$ be a point of the Singer line.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\varphi x\ne x$ (projectively), then there are exactly $r$ points $y\in P(L)\setminus\{x\}$ with $z/x=(y/x)^{\rho_1}$ for some $z\in P(L)$.
\item If $\varphi x=x$, there are none.
\end{itemize}
The number $e$ of $\varphi$-fixed points of $P(L)$ is $0$, $1$ or $2$ according as $r\equiv2$, $0$ or $1\pmod3$.
Consequently, if $m$ is prime, $N(\rho)=r\,(q+1-e)$ for each of the six ratios $\rho$ in the $S_3$-orbit of $\rho_1$; this orbit contains $\rho_0=r^4+r^{-4}=(r+1)r^4$.
If $r\equiv1\pmod3$ then $3\mid m$, so for prime $m$ we have $e\in\{0,1\}$, and $e=1$ exactly when $p=3$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(i) \emph{Reduction.} Put $u=y/x$. Then $y\in P(L)$ iff $\mathrm{Tr}(xu)=0$. If $x$ is not $\varphi$-fixed, then $\psi=\varphi(x)/x\notin F$ and
$\mathrm{Tr}(x\psi)=\mathrm{Tr}(\varphi x)=0$, so $\{u:\mathrm{Tr}(xu)=0\}=F+F\psi$.
In $K^\times/F^\times$ the exponent $\rho_1$ acts as $u\mapsto\tau(u)\tau^{-1}(u)$, so we need $z=x\,\tau(u)\,\tau^{-1}(u)\in F^\times L$. That is, $\mathrm{Tr}(x\,\tau(u)\,\tau^{-1}(u))=0$.
Take $u=1+s\psi$ with $s\in F$; on $F$ we have $\tau s=s^r$ and $\tau^{-1}s=s^{r^2}$. Using $\mathrm{Tr}(x)=0$, the condition reads $As^r+Bs^{r^2}+Cs^{r+r^2}=0$, where
$A=\mathrm{Tr}(x\tau\psi)$, $B=\mathrm{Tr}(x\tau^{-1}\psi)$ and $C=\mathrm{Tr}(x\,\tau\psi\,\tau^{-1}\psi)$ lie in $F$.
Remove $s=0$ (that is, $y=x$) and put $Z=s^{-r}$, with $s=\infty$ corresponding to $Z=0$. This gives the affine $\mathbb F_r$-linear equation
\[A Z^r+BZ+C=0,\qquad Z\in F.\]
(ii) \emph{Two identities.} Write $\xi_j=\tau^j(x)$ for $j\bmod 9$, with $\xi_j+\xi_{j+3}+\xi_{j+6}=0$. Then $A$, $B$, $C$ are rational functions of $\xi_0,\dots,\xi_5$
with integer coefficients and monomial denominators, and $\tau$ acts as the index shift. Let $D$ be the Dickson matrix of $f(Z)=AZ^r+BZ$. Exact computation in Sage gives
\[N_{F/\mathbb F_r}(A)+N_{F/\mathbb F_r}(B)=0\quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{adj}(D)\,(C,C^r,C^{r^2})^{T}=0.\]
After clearing the (nonzero) denominators, both are identities between polynomials with integer coefficients that hold over $\mathbb Q$. They therefore hold in every characteristic.
They were also re-checked directly over $\mathbb F_2,\mathbb F_3,\mathbb F_5,\mathbb F_7$.
(iii) \emph{Non-degeneracy.} The numerator of $A$ factors as $(\xi_1\xi_3-\xi_0\xi_4)(\xi_1^2+\xi_1\xi_4+\xi_4^2)$, and the denominator is $-\xi_1\xi_4\xi_7\ne0$.
The second factor vanishes iff $\psi$ is a root of $t^2+t+1$. The first vanishes iff $\tau x=cx$ with $c\in F$, which gives $\varphi x=N_{F/\mathbb F_r}(c)\,x$.
In both cases $x$ is $\varphi$-fixed. So $A\ne0$, hence $N(B)=-N(A)\ne0$ and $\mathrm{rank}\,D=2$.
(iv) \emph{Counting.} $N(-B/A)=N(-1)\,N(B)/N(A)=(-1)^{1+r+r^2}\cdot(-1)=1$, so the kernel of $f$ has exactly $r$ elements and $|\mathrm{Im}\,f|=r^2$.
Since $\mathrm{adj}(D)\,D=\det(D)\,I=0$, each row $(w_0,w_1,w_2)$ of $\mathrm{adj}(D)$ annihilates $(b,b^r,b^{r^2})$ for every $b\in\mathrm{Im}\,f$.
A nonzero row gives a nonzero linearised polynomial of degree at most $r^2$. Its zero set therefore has at most $r^2$ elements and contains $\mathrm{Im}\,f$, so it equals $\mathrm{Im}\,f$.
By (ii), $-C\in\mathrm{Im}\,f$, so there are exactly $r$ solutions $Z$, that is, $r$ partners $y$.
(v) \emph{Fixed points.} The $\varphi$-fixed points of $P(L)$ are the eigenlines of the $F$-linear map $\varphi|_L$, whose eigenvalues are cube roots of unity in $F$.
The eigenvalue $1$ needs $x\in F$, and then $\mathrm{Tr}(x)=3x$ vanishes only if $p=3$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $r\equiv2\pmod3$, then $F$ has no primitive cube root of unity, so $e=0$.
\item If $p=3$, then $\varphi|_L$ is unipotent with fixed line $F\cdot1$, so $e=1$.
\item If $r\equiv1\pmod3$, then $\varphi|_L$ has the two eigenvalues $\omega,\omega^2$ ($\omega\in F$ a primitive cube root of unity), so $e=2$.
\end{itemize}
For a fixed point:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\varphi x=\omega x$, then $K$ splits into $\varphi$-eigenlines, which $\tau$ preserves, so $\tau x=cx$ and $\tau^{-1}x=c'x$ with $c,c'\in F$, and $\{u:\mathrm{Tr}(xu)=0\}=F+Fx$.
For $u=\beta_0+\beta x$ the condition becomes $3cc'\beta^{r+r^2}x^3=0$, i.e.\ $\beta=0$, i.e.\ $y=x$.
\item If $p=3$, then $L=F+Fv$ with $\varphi v=v+\alpha$, $\alpha\in F^\times$. For $y=\beta_0+\beta v$, the condition reduces to $\mathrm{Tr}(v^2)\,\beta^{r+r^2}=0$ with $\mathrm{Tr}(v^2)=-\alpha^2\ne0$, so again $y=x$.
\end{itemize}
Summing over $x$ gives $N(\rho_1)$ when $m$ is prime, because then every nonzero difference is a unit.
The maps $(a,b,c)\mapsto(b,a,c)$ and $(a,b,c)\mapsto(a,c,b)$ realise $\rho\mapsto1-\rho$ and $\rho\mapsto1/\rho$ and preserve the unit conditions, so $N$ is constant on the $S_3$-orbit.
\end{proof}
The theorem was also checked by independent brute force for $r\le13$; for $q=8,27,512,1331$ it was checked on all six ratios. A separate AI review accepted it after the fixes included here.

\emph{Algebraic form.} The six ratios of the orbit are roots modulo $m$ of $x^3-3x+1$ and $x^3-3x^2+1$. For prime $m$ they are all of the roots;
for composite $m$ these cubics can have more roots, by the Chinese remainder theorem. The first is the minimal polynomial of $2\cos(2\pi/9)=\zeta_9+\zeta_9^{-1}$, with $\zeta_9\mapsto r$.
Its root set is invariant under $x\mapsto1/(1-x)$, the inverse of the order-3 map $s\mapsto1-1/s$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:curve}(a).

\section{Galois autocorrelation of Jacobi sums: exact values}\label{sec:gal}
Let $K=\mathbb F_{q^3}$ ($q$ an odd prime; Theorem~\ref{thm:gal} needs $q\ge5$), $m=q^2+q+1$, and let $L:K^\times\to\mathbb Z/m$ be the discrete logarithm modulo $m$; its kernel is
$\mathbb F_q^\times$. Let $\psi$ be a character of $K^\times$ of order $m$ (trivial on $\mathbb F_q^\times$) and put
$J[a,b]=\sum_{y\in K\setminus\{0,1\}}\psi^a(1-y)\psi^b(y)$, a Jacobi sum. For a unit $l$ modulo $m$ define the \emph{Galois autocorrelation}
\[C(l)=\sum_{a\ne0}\sum_{b}J[a,b]\,\overline{J[la,lb]},\]
i.e.\ the correlation of the Jacobi sums with their conjugates under $\zeta_m\mapsto\zeta_m^{\,l}$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:I3} below, these correlations are
the per-dilation third moments of Section~6 in another form. We also use the elementary count
\[P(l)=\#\{(y,c,d):\ y\in K\setminus\{0,1\},\ c,d\in\mathbb F_q^\times,\ c\,y^l+d\,(1-y)^l=1\}\]
(equivalently, pairs $y,y'$ with $L(1-y')=l\,L(1-y)$ and $L(y')=l\,L(y)$).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:gal}
$C(l)=m^2P(l)-((q-1)m-1)^2-(m-1)$ for every unit $l$, and $P(l)=P(l^{-1})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By orthogonality, $\sum_{a,b}J[a,b]\overline{J[la,lb]}=m^2\#\{(y,y'):L(1-y)=lL(1-y'),\,L(y)=lL(y')\}=m^2P(l^{-1})$, by the pair description of $P$; counting the same set of pairs with the other coordinate as the free variable gives $m^2P(l)$, hence
$P(l^{-1})=P(l)$. (The power $y^l$ depends on the integer representative of $l$ only up to a factor in $\mathbb F_q^\times$, which is absorbed into $(c,d)$.) The row $a=0$ equals $\sum_b J[0,b]\overline{J[0,lb]}$ with $J[0,0]=q^3-2=(q-1)m-1$ and $J[0,b]=-1$ for $b\ne0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:I3}
For every unit $l$ modulo $m$, $P(l)-(q-2)^2=\sum_{\mu}\bigl(I_l(\mu)\bigr)_3$, where $I_l(\mu)=|S\cap(lS+\mu)|$ as in Section~6.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Identify $\mathbb Z/m$ with the points of $\mathrm{PG}(2,q)=K^\times/\mathbb F_q^\times$ by the discrete logarithm; $S$ is a line, translation by $\mu$ is multiplication by $g^\mu$, and
dilation by $l$ is $x\mapsto x^l$. Because $l$ is a unit modulo $m$, this map is a bijection of the points, so it maps distinct points to distinct points (this is the only use of the
unit condition, and it is essential: for non-units the identity fails). Since the Singer group acts regularly on lines, $\sum_\mu(I_l(\mu))_3$ is the number $T_S$ of ordered
triples of distinct points of $S$ whose $l$-th powers are collinear. Multiplication by $g^\nu$ maps lines to lines and $(g^\nu x)^l=g^{l\nu}x^l$, so $T_{S'}=T_S$ for every line $S'$.
Counting the triples of distinct collinear points with collinear $l$-th powers in two ways (by their line, or by their first point, which multiplication moves to $1$ freely) gives
$m\,T_S=m\,T_1$, where $T_1$ is the number of ordered pairs $(x_2,x_3)$ of points such that $1,x_2,x_3$ are distinct and collinear and $1,x_2^l,x_3^l$ are collinear.
The map $y\mapsto([y],[1-y])$ is a bijection from $K\setminus\mathbb F_q$ onto the pairs $(x_2,x_3)$ with $1,x_2,x_3$ distinct and collinear: given such a pair, $1=a y_0+b z_0$ for
representatives $y_0,z_0$ and unique $a,b\in\mathbb F_q^\times$, and $y=a y_0$ is the unique representative of $x_2$ with $1-y$ representing $x_3$. Collinearity of the three distinct points
$1,y^l,(1-y)^l$ means that $c\,y^l+d(1-y)^l=1$ for exactly one $(c,d)\in(\mathbb F_q^\times)^2$. Hence the sum counts the solutions of $P(l)$ with $y\notin\mathbb F_q$. For
$y\in\mathbb F_q\setminus\{0,1\}$ there are exactly $q-2$ admissible pairs $(c,d)$, which gives the term $(q-2)^2$.
\end{proof}
The lemma was found by an AI referee and checked numerically in the release (\texttt{v5\_lemma\_I3.py}, $5\le q\le19$, ten values of $l$ each, with the controls $l=1$, where both sides
equal $q^3-q$, and $l=-1,2$, where both sides vanish). In particular $P(l)\ge(q-2)^2$ always. For $l=3$ and $l=-2$
the equation is a polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$ of degree $\le4$ in $y$, and its roots in $K\setminus\mathbb F_q$ come from irreducible cubic factors; hence
$P(l)=(q-2)^2+3I(l)$, where $I(l)$ is the number of $(c,d)\in(\mathbb F_q^\times)^2$ for which the polynomial has an irreducible cubic factor
($I$ is not the triple count $N(\rho)$ of the earlier sections).

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:gal}
Let $q\ge5$ be prime. Define $b_q=0$ if $q\equiv2\pmod3$ and $b_q=4x^2-2q$ if $q=x^2+3y^2$ $(q\equiv1\pmod3)$.
($b_q$ is the $q$-th coefficient of the weight-3 newform $\eta(2\tau)^3\eta(6\tau)^3$ of level $12$, which has CM by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$ \cite{LM}; this is the standard
Hecke-character description of a CM form, and we checked it for all primes $5\le q\le199$. It is not used below.)
\begin{itemize}
\item[(a)] $P(2)=P(-1)=(q-2)^2$, the least possible value. By Lemma~\ref{lem:I3} this is equivalent to the conic fact $(I_\lambda)_3=0$ of Section~6 \cite{JV,Pi}; we include it for completeness.
\item[(b)] If $q\equiv2\pmod3$, then $P(3)=(q-2)(2q-1)$.
\item[(c)] $I(-2)=\big((q-2)(q+1)+b_q\big)/3$, hence $P(-2)=(q-2)(2q-1)+b_q$. In particular $P(3)=P(-2)$ when $q\equiv2\pmod3$.
\end{itemize}
Equivalently (Lemma~\ref{lem:I3}), $\sum_\mu(I_3(\mu))_3=(q-2)(q+1)$ for $q\equiv2\pmod3$ and $\sum_\mu(I_{-2}(\mu))_3=(q-2)(q+1)+b_q$. (The unit condition: $l=\pm3$ is a unit modulo $m$ only for $q\equiv2\pmod3$.)
\end{theorem}
\noindent\emph{Corollary.} If $m$ is prime, then $\sum_{\rho\ne0,1}N(\rho)^2=\sum_{\lambda}\bigl(P(\lambda)-(q-2)^2\bigr)$, the sum over all units $\lambda$ (Section~6 and Lemma~\ref{lem:I3}).
So Conjecture~\ref{conj:var}, which is a sufficient condition for the order-3 case of the hypothesis of Proposition~\ref{prop:red}, is a statement about the average of $P(\lambda)$ over all $\lambda$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:gal} evaluates four of the roughly $q^2$ terms, and Theorem~\ref{thm:four} adds the class $\{4,\tfrac14\}$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of (a)]
For $l=2$ and $l=-1$ (after clearing denominators) the equation is a nonzero polynomial of degree $\le2$ over $\mathbb F_q$. Its roots lie in
$\mathbb F_{q^2}\cap K=\mathbb F_q$, so only the $(q-2)^2$ solutions with $y\in\mathbb F_q$ remain.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (b), sketch]
If $c=d$ the polynomial has degree $2$ and, as in (a), has no root outside $\mathbb F_q$. For $c\ne d$ write $c\,y^3+d(1-y)^3-1$ as $(c-d)\,g(y)$ with $g(y)=y^3+b(y^2-y)+e$. The map $(c,d)\mapsto(b,e)$ is a bijection onto the pairs with
$b\notin\{0,-3\}$ and $b\ne3e$. On the excluded lines, $g$ has a root in $\mathbb F_q$, since cubing is bijective on $\mathbb F_q$. In the family
$x^3+b(x^2-x)+e$, two distinct roots determine the third, except when the two roots sum to $1$, which needs $a^2-a+1=0$; this has no solution because
$-3$ is a non-square. Counting triple roots ($2$), double roots ($q-3$), ordered pairs of roots and root incidences gives
$(q^2-4q+7)/6$ split, $(q^2+1)/2$ one-root and $(q-2)(q+1)/3$ irreducible members. Hence $I(3)=(q-2)(q+1)/3$ and $P(3)=(q-2)^2+3I(3)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of (c), outline]
The full proof is in the release (file \texttt{N2\_PROOF.md}); we list the steps. Let $\phi$ be the quadratic character of $\mathbb F_q$.
(1) For distinct roots $y_1,y_2\in\mathbb F_q\setminus\{0,1\}$ the two root conditions are linear in $(c,d)$, with determinant
$(y_2-y_1)(y_1+y_2-2y_1y_2)$, $c=y_1^2y_2^2(y_2-y_1)(2-y_1-y_2)/\det$ and $d=(1-y_1)^2(1-y_2)^2(y_2^2-y_1^2)/\det$.
Put $u=y_1+y_2$ and $v=y_1y_2$.
(2) The number of admissible unordered pairs is $P_2=(q-3)(q-5)/2$.
(3) The residual quadratic of the quartic has discriminant $u(u-2)(2v-u+2)/(2v-u)$. Summing its quadratic character over admissible pairs gives
$S_2=(T-4q+14)/2$ with $T=\sum_{x,z}\phi\big((x^2-1)(z^2-1)(x^2+1-2z)\big)$. The excluded lines contribute $2q-4$, $2q-6$, $-3$ and $0$.
(4) A change of variables gives $T=1+\phi(-1)G(4)$, where $G(\lambda)=\sum_{x,y}\phi\big(xy(1-x)(1-y)(x-\lambda y)\big)$, and $q^2\,{}_3F_2(4)=\phi(-1)G(4)$
for Greene's ${}_3F_2$ with quadratic characters.
(5) The repeated-root types have counts $E_1=(q+1-z_0)/2$ (type $(1)^2(2)$), $E_2=(q-5-z_0)/2$ (type $(1)(1)(1)^2$) and $E_3=z_0/2$ (type $(1)^2(1)^2$), where $z_0=1+\phi(-3)$. A double root $a$ forces $c=a^3$ and $d=-(a-1)^3$.
(6) Counting roots, pairs and character-weighted pairs by factorisation type gives
$I(-2)=(q-2)^2-E_1-\tfrac43P_2+\tfrac23S_2-\tfrac13E_2-\tfrac43E_3$.
(7) The value $q^2\,{}_3F_2(4)=q+b_q$ follows from \cite[Thm.~1.7]{BK} (Greene's ${}_3F_2$ \cite{Gre}, character $S=\phi$, allowed there since $\phi$ has order $2$); the authors of \cite{BK}
state that $S=\phi$ gives a result of Ono \cite{On}. For $q\equiv2\pmod 3$ the value is $1/q$.
For $q\equiv1\pmod3$ the theorem gives $[(\phi\chi_3\text{ over }\chi_3)+(\phi\chi_3^2\text{ over }\chi_3^2)]^2-1/q$; here the Hasse--Davenport relation \cite{IR} gives
$J(\phi\chi_3,\bar\chi_3)=\chi_3(2)\overline{J(\chi_3,\chi_3)}$, and the cubic character of $2$ \cite{IR} makes the real part $\pm x$, so $q^2\,{}_3F_2(4)=4x^2-q=q+b_q$.
Substituting (2)--(7) into (6) gives (c).
\end{proof}

after that review; it was checked numerically and by the AI referee reports prepared for Version~5, which corrected one sentence of the released proof (the choice of associate). None of these is a human expert review.

\emph{Geometric explanation (heuristic, not used in the proof).} In the twisted form, the level sets of $\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha)\mathrm{Tr}(\alpha^{-1})$ are the members of the pencil
$(x+y)(y+z)(z+x)=k\,xyz$. This pencil is the extremal rational elliptic surface with singular fibres $I_6,I_3,I_2,I_1$, i.e.\ the universal curve for $\Gamma_1(6)$.
The fibre types were computed exactly. We expect the double cover branched at the $I_3$ and $I_1$ fibres to be the singular K3 surface with fibres $3I_6+3I_2$ and transcendental
lattice $\mathrm{diag}(2,6)$ \cite{HS}, which has complex multiplication by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, but we have not written down the isomorphism. This would explain why the error term is a CM coefficient. (The K3 surface of Section~\ref{sec:four} is a different surface, attached to $\lambda=4$, and does not supply this isomorphism.)

\emph{Observations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $P(4)=P(-2)$, i.e.\ $\sum_\mu(I_4)_3=\sum_\mu(I_{-2})_3$, for all 20 primes $5\le q\le79$ tested; for primes $q\ge7$ this is now proved (Theorem~\ref{thm:four}; by a computer-assisted computation of both sides, not by a symmetry of $P$). Besides $P(ql)=P(l)$ (Frobenius) and $P(l^{-1})=P(l)$, a search over M\"obius maps with integer coefficients in $[-3,3]$ found no further symmetry of $P$ ($q=11,\dots,23$), so this equality is not explained by such a map.
\item Among the rationals $a/b$ with $|a|,|b|\le12$ that are units modulo $m$ for every tested $q$ ($11\le q\le41$; this excludes all $a/b$ involving $3$ or $7$), the only classes of equal $P$ beyond $\{l,l^{-1}\}$ are $\{2,-1,\tfrac12\}$ and $\{-2,-\tfrac12,4,\tfrac14\}$. For $q\equiv2\pmod3$ the second class also contains $3$ and $\tfrac13$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}(b),(c).
\item For other small heights, $(P(l)-(q-2)^2-q^2)/q$ fluctuates more as the height grows: over the primes $47\le q\le79$ (only $4$ to $8$ values of $q$ for each $l$) its standard deviation is about $1$ at $l=-2$ and about $4$ at $l=7$, but it is not monotone in the height, so this is a rough observation (smaller $q$ include multiplier coincidences such as $l=q$, which inflate it). This is consistent with cohomology that grows with the height, so these exact formulas do not by themselves give the averaged bound of Section~\ref{sec:rem}.
\item $N(\rho)$ is an average of root counts of the trinomials $c\,y^\rho+y-1$. Kelley proved, under GRH, that the root counts of random-coefficient trinomials over prime fields are Poisson, and conjectured the same for a fixed prime \cite{Ke,KO,BCR}. The Poisson-type laws of Sections~7--8 are an extension-field analogue in a different regime.
\end{itemize}

\section{The multiplier $4$}\label{sec:four}
Version~5 observed $P(4)=P(-2)$ for all primes $5\le q\le79$ (Section~\ref{sec:gal}). We now prove the corresponding formula for all prime powers of characteristic at least $7$.
Note that $4$ is a unit modulo $m=q^2+q+1$, since $m$ is odd.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:four}
Let $q=p^n$ with $p\ge7$ prime. Let $b_p=4x^2-2p$ if $p=x^2+3y^2$ and $b_p=0$ if $p\equiv2\pmod3$, let $\alpha,\beta$ be the roots of $X^2-b_pX+\bigl(\tfrac{-3}{p}\bigr)p^2$, and put $b_q=\alpha^n+\beta^n$. Then
\[\sum_\mu\bigl(I_4(\mu)\bigr)_3=(q-2)(q+1)+b_q,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P(4)=(q-2)(2q-1)+b_q .\]
In particular $P(4)=P(-2)$ for every prime $q\ge7$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}(c).
\end{theorem}
For prime $q$ this $b_q$ is the one of Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}. For $q=p^n$ it is the trace of $\mathrm{Frob}_q$ on the two-dimensional Galois representation attached to the newform $12.3.c.a$ \cite{LM}, which is not the
coefficient $a_{p^n}$. For $p\equiv2\pmod3$ it equals $2q$ for even $n$ and $0$ for odd $n$.

\begin{proof}[Proof, outline]
The proofs are in the release: \texttt{proofs/P4\_FULL\_PROOF.md} (overview), \texttt{P4\_PROOF.md} (steps), \texttt{P4\_CB\_PROOF.md} (the K3 surface) and \texttt{P4\_SPECIAL\_LEMMA.md} (the special classes). Step (5) is released as a proof sketch whose exceptional-point lists were generated and checked by computer; it is the least completely written part. Let $\chi$ be the quadratic character of $\mathbb F_q$ and $\phi(a)=\chi(a)$ for $a\in\mathbb F_p$ (the notation of the release).

(1) \emph{Reduction.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:I3} the claim is $P(4)-(q-2)^2=(q-2)(q+1)+b_q$. With $t=y/(1-y)$ the members of the family are $c\,t^4+d=(1+t)^4$. As in Section~\ref{sec:gal}, roots in $K\setminus\mathbb F_q$ come from irreducible cubic factors. The number of such members is an explicit combination of the factorial moments $N_k=\sum_{c,d}(r)_k$ ($k=1,2,3$) of the number $r$ of roots of $c\,y^4+d(1-y)^4=1$ in $\mathbb F_q$, plus corrections for the degenerate members ($c+d=0$) and for repeated roots, which are proved by hand. Also by hand, $N_1=(q-2)^2+2(q-1)$ and $N_2=q^2-4q+10+5\phi(-1)$. The theorem is then equivalent to
\[N_3=q^2-7q+19+18\phi(-1)+9\phi(-2)+6\bigl(1+\phi(-3)\bigr)+b_q.\tag{N3}\]

(2) \emph{Main term.} Ordered pairs of distinct roots $(t_1,t_2)$ correspond to $(u,v)=(t_1+t_2,t_1t_2)$ with weight $1+\chi(u^2-4v)$. The other two roots are those of a residual quadratic, whose discriminant is $-4D_4(u,v)$ up to a square. Hence $N_3=q^2+\phi(-1)(S_a+S_b)+E$, where $S_a=\sum\chi(D_4)$, $S_b=\sum\chi(u^2-4v)\chi(D_4)$, and $E$ collects the pairs where this parametrisation degenerates.

(3) \emph{Character sums.} The substitution $u=x-1$, $v=z+x^2/4-x/2$, $m'=1-4z$ gives $D_4=H(x^2,m')/4$ with
$H(s,m)=m^3+8m^2-3ms^2+6ms+13m+2s^3+2s^2-10s+6$, which splits $S_a$ and $S_b$ into four sums. Three of them are evaluated by hand (projection from a node of the curve $H=0$): $A=(1+\phi(-1))q+\chi(3)$, $A'=2q$, $B'=(1+\phi(-1))q+\chi(3)$. The fourth is
$B=\sum_{s,m}\chi\bigl(s\,m\,H(s,m)\bigr)$. Projection from the node $(1,-4)$ gives $B=q-1+\sum_{k}\sum_{x}\chi\bigl(x(x-e_1)(x-e_2)\bigr)$ with $e_1=(k-1)^2(k+2)(k+4)$ and $e_2=k(k-2)(k+3)^2$. This is a point count on the elliptic K3 surface
$X:\ Y^2=X'(X'-e_1)(X'-e_2)$ over the $k$-line, whose singular fibres are $2I_4+5I_2+I_6$.

(4) \emph{The K3 surface.} Exact canonical heights of eleven explicit sections \cite{Shi} give Mordell--Weil rank at least $2$, hence $\rho(X)=20$ since $\rho\le h^{1,1}=20$; the discriminant of the span is $3$ times a square, so $\mathrm{End}_{\rm Hodge}(T)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$ ($X$ has complex multiplication by $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$). The sections are defined over $\mathbb Q(i)$, and complex conjugation acts on their span by $\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)$.
$X$ has good reduction at every $p\ge7$: the eight singular places are distinct modulo $p$, and the local constants at the nodes are $\{2,3,5\}$-units, so the Weierstrass model has a simultaneous resolution over $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$.
The Lefschetz trace formula then gives $B=2q+\phi(-1)q+\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_q\,|\,T)$, where $T$ is the transcendental part of $H^2$. By Livn\'e \cite{Li95}, $T$ is modular (see also \cite{Sch09}). Its determinant is $\varepsilon_K\cdot\chi_{\rm cyc}^2$, $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$: $G_K$ acts through $\mathrm{SO}(T)$ because it commutes with the CM action, and elements outside $G_K$ act by reflections (this short argument is in \texttt{P4\_CB\_PROOF.md}, Step 5, and has not been checked by an expert). Its traces are even (the full rational $2$-torsion makes every smooth-fibre trace even).
By Livn\'e's criterion \cite[Thm.~4.3 and Prop.~4.11]{Li87} with bad set $\{2,3,5\}$ (for this set, equal traces at the primes $\le73$ suffice, as in \cite[\S2.7--2.8]{Sch12}; our test set, which also contains $83$ and $241$, is larger), and an exact check that $\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_p\,|\,T)=\bigl(\tfrac3p\bigr)b_p$ for $7\le p\le420$, we get $\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Frob}_q\,|\,T)=\chi(3)\,b_q$. Hence $B=2q+\phi(-1)q+\chi(3)b_q$.

(5) \emph{Exclusion term.} $E$ is a sum over explicit categories of pairs. All of them except one are evaluated by hand. The exception is the contribution $\mathrm{SPEC}$ of three special classes of members ($c=1$, $c+d=0$, repeated roots). Each class lies on a smooth conic on which $-D_4$ is the square of a rational function. The weights are therefore constant outside finite exceptional sets, all defined over $L=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3},\sqrt{-7})$, whose points collide modulo $p$ only for $p\in\{2,3,5,7,13\}$.
Hence $\mathrm{SPEC}=A(\sigma)q+B(\sigma)$, where $A(\sigma)$ and $B(\sigma)$ depend only on $\sigma=\mathrm{Frob}_q\in\mathrm{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$ (for $p=7,13$, only on $n\bmod2$). The required values follow from exact computation at two $q$ for each case: all $16$ elements of $\mathrm{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$ occur at least twice among the verified primes; for $p=7$ we use $q=7,343$ ($n$ odd) and $49,2401$ ($n$ even); for $p=13$ we use $13,2197$ and $169,28561$.

Combining (2)--(5) gives (N3), and with (1) the theorem.
\end{proof}

\emph{Status.} This proof is computer-assisted. Exact computation (Sage, SymPy, NumPy) supplies: the canonical heights and the Galois action on the sections, the Livn\'e test primes, the lists of exceptional points, several polynomial identities, and the finite verification in step (5). The proof files record five AI referee reviews (referee 2 on reproducibility, the others on mathematics), run on a different model from the one that wrote the proof; they found no fatal error, and their minor points are fixed (their findings are recorded in the proof files; report 5 is released in full). Two further AI referee reports reviewed this version of the paper. It has not been reviewed by a human expert. It has not been reviewed by a human expert.

\emph{Verification.} (N3) holds exactly at all 2259 primes $7\le q\le19999$, with no failure (666 primes $7\le q\le4999$ recomputed by \texttt{scripts/v6\_counts.py}, and the Kaggle outputs for $5000\le q\le19999$). The closed form was found from the primes below $140$; the $43$ primes $7\le q\le199$ were checked before the formula was registered, and the primes from $211$ to $19999$ after.
For $p\ge7$, (N3) also holds at the prime powers $q=49,121,169,343,2197,2401,28561$ (\texttt{scripts/v6\_pp.json}; these include the parity checks at $p=7,13$ used in step (5)). The third moment $\sum_\mu(I_4)_3$ computed directly from Singer sets equals $(q-2)(q+1)+b_q$ at $q=49,121$ (and, in characteristic $5$, at $q=25,125$; registered before computing).

\emph{Characteristics $3$ and $5$ (observed, not proved).} For $p=5$ the formula of Theorem~\ref{thm:four} holds at $q=5,25,125$ for the third moment, and (N3) holds for $q=5^n$, $1\le n\le7$ (\texttt{scripts/ch5\_small.json}, \texttt{v6\_pp.json}, \texttt{kaggle\_outputs}). The surface $X$ has bad reduction at $5$, and the sum $B$ alone does not follow the formula there.
For $p=3$ we observe $\sum_\mu(I_4)_3=q\,(q-2+(-1)^n)$ for $q=9,27,\dots,729$ (the values at $243$ and $729$ were registered before computing).

\section{What remains}\label{sec:rem}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:red}, a negative answer to the $O(1)$ question would follow from Poisson convergence of all fixed-order factorial moments of $Z$, that is, $R_k\to0$ for each fixed $k$ and each $C$.
This is a full Poisson limit law for the window counts, not a single remaining estimate. For $k=3$ it reduces to a variance bound: Conjecture~\ref{conj:var}, or any bound $o(q^4/\log q)$ on the variance,
and by the Corollary of Section~\ref{sec:gal} this is a statement about the average of $P(\lambda)$ over all units $\lambda$. For $k\ge4$ we have no comparable reduction. In
Fourier terms the missing input for $k=3$ is cancellation, on average over the dilation parameter, in correlations of Jacobi sums over
$\mathbb F_{q^3}$, beyond the square-root (Weil--Deligne) bound for single sums. We do not know how to prove it. Section~\ref{sec:gal} evaluates these correlations exactly at a few small dilations. Three cheap routes to the averaged bound failed in our tests: the exact formulas grow in complexity with the height of the dilation; among small heights (numerators and denominators $\le12$, avoiding $3$ and $7$) we found no further coincidences of $P$ beyond those explained by Theorem~\ref{thm:gal} and one unexplained class; and maximum bounds for roots of trinomials \cite{BCR} are far too weak for an $L^2$ statement.

\section{Limitations, literature and disclosure}\label{sec:lim}
Theorem~\ref{thm:curve} covers fixed ratios only (prime $q$, or large characteristic). The higher-moment and variance laws are
numerical. Theorem~\ref{thm:res} is proved; two polynomial identities and one factorisation in its proof were verified by exact computer algebra (Sage 10.7; the scripts and their SHA-256 hashes are released) rather than by hand. The excess formula of Section~\ref{sec:resblind} is a heuristic, not a theorem. For Version~3, a search (web, zbMATH Open, three full texts; 20 October 2026) found no prior statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:res}. Proposition~\ref{prop:orb} is elementary and probably known. Proposition~\ref{prop:red} is conditional on moment convergence, which is not proved. A full-text literature review and a
zbMATH Open search (19 October 2026) found the forbidden ratios, the arc and oval facts \cite{JV,Pi} and the dilate strategy
(\cite{Go}, Shakan \cite{Sh}). They did not find the curve count, the two laws, the identity of Section~6 or the reduction of
Section~4. Guy's C9 entry and MathSciNet were not checked. Computations and drafting were done with AI assistance (Anthropic Claude).
The statements labelled as proved were checked by separate AI instances and by exact computation, but not yet by a human expert. For Version~4 (searches on 30 October 2026: zbMATH Open, web, GitHub), we found no prior statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}(b),(c) or of Proposition~\ref{prop:gal}; Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}(a) is, by Lemma~\ref{lem:I3}, equivalent to the classical conic fact \cite{JV,Pi}. Also not checked for Version~4: the original paper of Jungnickel and Vedder, the full text of the paper of Ono, and a 2005 essay on Singer sets (A.~Chmelnitzki), whose server was unreachable. Theorem~\ref{thm:gal}(c) relies on \cite[Thm.~1.7]{BK} (statement read; a preprint; we did not confirm a journal version) and on standard facts from \cite{IR}. All registrations in this paper are self-attested (Section~1). For Version~6 (searches on 4 November 2026: web, arXiv, two of three MathOverflow phrasings; the third was blocked by a verification page) we found no prior statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:four}.
